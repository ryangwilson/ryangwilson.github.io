overview: A client came in with an idea and wanted to build the "Uber for Trucking." He had a problem he wanted to solve but didn't know where to start, and the timeline was short&#58; three months, ending with a trade show where he wanted to unveil the product and start signing up users.<br> <br>I owned product design and research, working closely alongside a Tech Lead and one additional engineer who built the application.
problemstatement: There is a problem in the trucking industry that is specifically painful to independent truckers and small trucking companies. When driving a fully loaded trailer from, for example, Chicago to Dallas, there is no efficient way to book your trailer to be refilled on your return trip to Chicago. Driving an empty trailer, called 'deadheading,' is costly for two reasons; you have to pay for the gas to get back home, and there is no load to deliver and get paid for when you arrive home.
goals: <ul><li>Conceptualize a concept that would alleviate pain points for independent truckers</li><li>Have the product in the Google Play and Apple App Store</li><li>Sign up users in-app at an upcoming trade show</li><li>Deliver an MVP in three months</li></ul>
role: Product Designer
scope: Twelve Weeks (team of three, made up of myself, a Tech Lead, and one additional engineer)
tools: Research, Observation, Documentation, Usability Testing, Illustrator, Axure, Stache, HTML, LESS
methods: 
process: Every project starts with a shared source of truth&#58; a repository holding the design rationale and a glossary of industry terms like 'deadheading' and 'LTL' (less than load), so the whole team, including anyone who joins later, is speaking the same language from day one.<br> <br>Understanding the idea meant seeing it from two vantage points&#58; the stakeholder's, and the drivers who'd actually use it. I spent two full days workshopping the concept with the stakeholder to capture his thinking, then interviewed eight independent truck drivers and logistics experts to understand their process and pain points firsthand. Those conversations became six personas representing the product's core user types.<br> <br>The interviews also surfaced a feature nobody had considered going in&#58; more than half the drivers I spoke with brought up, unprompted, how much paper they collect in their cabs from pickups and deliveries, feedback that led directly to proposing a paperless solution to the stakeholder.
findings: With that research in hand, I could start defining the product&#58; what to build now, what to hold for later, and how to get there. I drafted scenarios grounded in what I'd heard, vetted them with the stakeholder and his industry contacts, then used them to bring the rest of the team up to speed on what the product actually needed to do.<br> <br>Product flows came next&#58; the clearest way to work through how each piece of the product fit together. Mapping it out let me establish real requirements from it, and both the lead developer and stakeholder vetted them to make sure everyone was aligned before moving forward.

findings-continued: Mockups came with a tension I always try to manage&#58; go high-fidelity too early and a stakeholder gets anchored on color and icons before the interaction itself is even tested. The short timeline overrode that instinct here — I moved to high fidelity quickly so the team had a real sense of the aesthetic to build toward, and so I could hand developers a style guide sooner rather than later.<br> <br>Each iteration went through usability testing, using multiple Axure prototypes rather than a simple click-through, since Axure simulates real interactivity and data input more closely. I tested whether users could complete four core tasks, measuring success by completion rate and time to completion&#58; <ul><li>Setting up an account</li><li>Finding and booking a return load</li><li>Booking a partial less than load</li><li>Finding shipper details like contact information and pick up location</li></ul><br> <br>The results shaped real interface decisions&#58; how to group inputs, and that retaining a driver's trailer details after their first booking would speed up every booking after it, since those details rarely change. Users could post their availability in an average of one minute forty-nine seconds on their first try, and once a lane was booked, they could quickly accept it and get to the shipper's contact information and pickup location.
processlist: <ul><li>Research</li><li>Workshops</li><li>User interviews</li><li>Product Flows</li><li>Sketches</li><li>Wireframes</li><li>Design System</li><li>Mockups</li><li>Prototypes</li><li>Usability Testing</li><li>Iteration & Refinement</li></ul>
solutions: Once usability testing was complete, I turned to supporting the development team directly&#58; writing Stache (semantic HTML) for the app pages and styling the UI in LESS.<br> <br>Success came down to two things&#58; the product was live in the Google Play and App stores the day before the trade show, and it shipped with every intended Version One feature. Users could create accounts and start backfilling trailers on day one, and the stakeholder had a fully functioning app to promote and sign up users with at the trade show.
outcomes: In three months, we took the stakeholder's idea from concept to MVP, live in the Google Play and App stores before the first day of the trade show. That gave him something real to promote at the show, capture signups with, and use to raise more seed money for the project.<br> <br>In addition to my product design work I&#58;<ul><li>Refined the HaulHound logo</li><li>Created all in-app icons</li><li>Prepared App and Play store graphics</li><li>Developed marketing and presentation materials</li><li>Created trade show backdrops</li><li>Designed motion graphics and promotional videos for display at the trade show</li></ul>
achievements: <ul><li>Provided a solution for users to backfill their trailers</li> <li>Gave users the ability to combat "deadheading"</li><li>I worked in tandem with the Lead Developer to vet concepts and align expectations for the build. I made myself available for clarification outside of the documentation and chipped in by writing HTML (Stache) and CSS (LESS) for the project</li><li>I established a great relationship with the stakeholder, making it easy to reach out for answers and to get confirmation on direction quickly</li></ul>
takeaways: If I could do this project again, I would have stayed ugly longer&#58; wireframe, no color, until the interaction itself was settled. I never intended the orange to stick; I used it as a filler color until I'd defined a real palette, but the stakeholder bought into it early, and we ended up locked into that color scheme. This is the project that reinforced "stay ugly as long as you can" for me&#58; since then, I wait to share high-fidelity mockups with stakeholders until a color palette is locked in.
learnings: The short timeline was the biggest challenge on this project. Staying in frequent communication with the lead developer kept things moving smoothly, and the strong relationship I built with the stakeholder, who trusted the value I was providing, made it easy to get quick answers and confirmation on direction.
